There's a sentence you hear constantly: _"I work in AI"_ or _"We use AI"_ or _"I'm learning AI."_

It sounds like everyone is talking about the same thing. They're not.

> Using a bicycle, a cargo ship, and a Formula 1 car are all "transportation" — but they require completely different skills, serve different purposes, and belong to different worlds. AI is the same.

## The Three Faces of AI

Your relationship with AI determines which discipline you're in. The word "AI" is used to describe at least three completely different activities — and confusing them is the source of a lot of bad career advice, hype, and frustration.

![The 3 Layers of AI](/assets/3-layers-of-AI.svg)

### Layer 1 — Using AI

This is you, talking to ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. You're a user of a finished product.

The skill here is _prompting_ — knowing how to ask the right question, give the right context, and evaluate the response. It's genuinely useful to be good at this. But it's a skill closer to knowing how to operate a power tool than to building one.

You don't need to understand how neural networks work. You don't need to know what a transformer is. You need judgment and craft.

### Layer 2 — Building Software With AI

This is the developer who integrates an LLM into their product. The product itself isn't AI — it might be a customer support bot, a code review tool, or a document summarizer. The LLM is a component, like a database or an API.

Here you need software engineering skills plus an understanding of how LLMs behave: their latency, their cost, their tendency to hallucinate, their context limits. You're building _with_ AI, not _building_ AI.

Most software engineering jobs that mention "AI" today belong in this layer.

### Layer 3 — Building AI

This is training a model. You have a problem — detecting fraud, classifying medical images, predicting churn — and you build a model trained on your specific data to solve it.

This is a different discipline entirely: statistics, linear algebra, data pipelines, model evaluation, experiment tracking. The gap between Layer 2 and Layer 3 is enormous. Using an API and training a model are as different as driving a car and building an engine.

## Wait — Can They Overlap?

Yes — and this is where it gets interesting.

You can use a Layer 1 tool (ChatGPT) to help you do Layer 3 work (training a model). You can ask Claude to explain backpropagation, help debug your PyTorch code, or suggest architectures for your dataset.

But the tool doesn't replace the engineering. Using an LLM to help write the code that trains your fraud detection model doesn't make the task simpler — it still requires the same depth of knowledge. You're just using Layer 1 to move faster in Layer 3.

The layers can assist each other. They cannot substitute for each other.

## Precision Is the Point

The word "AI" has become so broad it's almost useless. When someone says they're "getting into AI," they might mean they're learning to write better prompts, or they're building API-powered apps, or they're pursuing an ML research career.

These are not the same path. They don't share the same prerequisites, the same job market, or the same learning curve.

Be more precise about which layer you're in — or which one you want to be in. That precision won't just help others understand what you're talking about. It'll help _you_ understand what you actually need to learn.
